Step 3. Your trade-offs

What research says about short programs and stacking credentials

These are averages from studies of specific groups in specific years. They do not predict what will happen to you. They are here so you know what questions to ask.

  • Many short-certificate earners do come back. In a study of California community college students who earned short-term certificates, 23 percent earned another credential within three years. (Public Policy Institute of California, 2018.) That means most did not, so continuing is a choice to plan for, not a given.
  • Stacking helps some people more than others. A national review described the earnings effect of earning more than one credential as "weakly positive and inconsistent." (Community College Research Center, Working Paper 97, 2017.) The same review found no clear evidence about noncredit awards in a stack.
  • What the second credential is matters. A Virginia study of credit-bearing programs found larger gains when the second credential was an associate degree, and a second certificate showed no significant effect. Staying in the same broad field did better than repeating the same specific program. (Meyer, Bird, and Castleman.)
  • Health programs vary a lot. A study of California community college health programs found that registered nursing and dental hygiene nearly doubled earnings within six years, while some shorter nursing and medical assistant programs did not show significant gains. More than half of students who started with a short-term health award came back within three years, but only about 20 percent finished a higher health award. (Public Policy Institute of California, October 2025.)
  • Noncredit programs vary by field. A study of about 128,000 Texas noncredit students found average gains of about $2,000 a year, with larger gains in fields such as construction and engineering technology and little or none in business and information sciences. It was done in Texas, not California. (Bahr and Columbus, 2025.)
  • Credit that counts is worth asking about. The review above found noncredit programs rarely lead into degree programs unless credit is given for them. That is why this tool asks whether the credit from each step counts toward the next.

This tool is for planning. It does not predict your future, decide whether you qualify for anything, or replace talking with a counselor or advisor. Pay and cost numbers are the ones you entered.
